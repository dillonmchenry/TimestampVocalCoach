# Vocal Style and Genre in Singing Coaching

Musical style and genre context fundamentally shape which vocal behaviours are aspirational,
acceptable, or problematic. A coaching system that ignores genre context will generate
misleading feedback — flagging stylistic features as errors, or missing genre-specific
technical expectations.

## The Role of the Reference Recording

In popular music coaching, a reference recording by the original artist provides the stylistic
ground truth for a song. Scoops, vibrato choices, dynamic approaches, and ornamentation that
appear in the original are stylistic features to be matched or deliberately deviated from —
not universal errors to be corrected.

This distinction between absolute technical quality (pitch accuracy, timing, breath support —
independent of style) and comparative style matching (how closely the singer replicates the
reference's specific choices) is central to interpretable feedback.

## Genre-Specific Vocal Norms

**Pop**: Emphasis on intimacy, direct communication, and expressive naturalness. Straight tone
often used in verses for intimacy; vibrato added in choruses or on key emotional words.
Scoops, slides, and stylistic intonation bends are common and expected in contemporary pop.
Breath tone (incomplete glottal closure) is frequently used for intimacy. Belt register
(chest-dominant mix through the upper range) is the hallmark of pop musical theatre.

**Rock**: Full, mixed registration through a wide range. Vibrato usage varies widely — from
straight tone (indie/alternative) to wide classical vibrato (classic rock). Distortion, grit,
and air in the tone are stylistic features, not phonatory problems. Rhythmic intensity and
placement on top of or behind the beat both have style-specific applications.

**Blues/Soul**: Scoops, falls, bends, and microtonal inflections are central stylistic features.
Blue notes (intentional flat thirds and sevenths) are not pitch errors. Wide vibrato and
expressive inconsistency are features of the tradition. Straight tone versus vibrato choices
are interpretive, not technical.

**Jazz**: Extreme stylistic variety. Vibrato may be wide (swing era), narrow, or absent
(modern/cool jazz). Timing is frequently laid back, with significant rubato and rhythmic
flexibility. Pitch bends, falls, and approach notes are stylistic ornaments, not errors.

**Classical/Opera**: Strict pitch accuracy expectations. Vibrato is the default sustained tone.
Passaggio management and dynamic range are primary technical concerns. Scoops and slides are
generally undesirable in classical technique unless explicitly indicated as portamenti.

**Musical Theatre (Contemporary Commercial Music)**: Wide range of vocal demands. Mix voice
and belt are primary technical requirements. Stylistic expression is valued; pitch accuracy
is expected throughout. Dynamic contrast between sections is highly prized.

**Folk**: Clean tone with limited vibrato. Straight, direct delivery is the stylistic norm.
Pitch accuracy is important but micro-inflections and slides are acceptable in folk idiom.
Breath tone in intimate passages is common and intentional.

## Using Genre Context in Coaching

When a vocal profile identifies a song's genre, the coaching system can:

1. **De-emphasise highlight types** that flag stylistic features as errors in that genre
   (e.g., scoop_habit in blues or jazz should be treated as a comparative note, not a flaw).

2. **Emphasise highlight types** that are especially important for that genre
   (e.g., vibrato_quality is more critical in classical than in indie folk).

3. **Adjust the coaching framing** to match the genre's technical priorities
   (e.g., breath support framing is different in opera versus pop).

4. **Interpret reference deviations** correctly — a singer deviating from a jazz reference
   by being too metronomic is more important feedback than if they slightly rush a pop song.

## Artist-Specific Context

Beyond genre, individual artists have distinctive vocal signatures. The coaching context
for a Beatles song differs from a Freddie Mercury song even if both are rock. Artist-level
context enriches the stylistic frame beyond genre generalisations, providing more specific
guidance about which features should be emphasised or understood as idiomatic.
