# Phrasing and Timing in Singing

Rhythmic placement — when within the beat a note begins and ends — is as musically significant
as pitch accuracy. Phrasing and timing encompass where notes sit relative to the accompaniment,
whether the singer leads, matches, or lags the pulse, and how these choices create different
expressive effects.

## The Mechanics of Onset Timing

Vocal note onset is controlled by two processes: breath impulse (subglottic pressure increases
to phonation threshold) and glottal closure (vocal folds adduct). For a note to begin on the
beat, both processes must be initiated slightly before the beat — anticipation is required
because both processes have a finite ramp-up time.

Habitual late entrances usually indicate that the singer is waiting to hear the beat before
initiating phonation, rather than anticipating it. Pre-loading the breath before the beat
arrival, and training the onset response as a reflex rather than a reaction, addresses
this systematically.

## Timing Consistency vs. Random Error

When timing analysis reveals that a singer's entrances are consistently early or consistently
late across a phrase window (rather than randomly scattered), the pattern reflects a systemic
mismatch between the singer's internal tempo and the track tempo. This is a different problem
from individual entrances that are late due to reaction time or inattention.

Consistent early entrances (rushing) often indicate performance anxiety: the singer's stress
response accelerates their internal clock. Consistent late entrances (dragging) may reflect
overcaution, unfamiliarity with the tempo, or a stylistic preference for "laying back."

The distinction matters for practice: random errors benefit from attention and
mindfulness; systematic drift benefits from tempo recalibration exercises against a metronome.

## Expressive Timing and Rubato

Rubato — deliberate temporal flexibility that deviates from strict metronomic time — is a
primary tool of expressive singing. A singer who gives the downbeat of the final phrase an
extra half-beat before resolving, or who pushes slightly ahead of the beat in an energetic
chorus, is making expressive timing choices that would register as "inaccurate" in a purely
numerical analysis.

Reference recording comparison is essential for distinguishing expressive timing from errors:
if the original performer also delays or rushes at that point, the singer's timing may be
stylistically appropriate rather than inaccurate. This is why feedback systems should
present timing data relative to the reference, not just relative to the metronome.

## Phrase-Level Timing Patterns

**Rushing a phrase**: All notes in a window arrive early. Common in phrases that feel
exposed, uncertain, or that a singer is anxious to get through. Practice at reduced tempo
until the phrase feels comfortable at performance speed.

**Dragging a phrase**: All notes arrive late. Common in phrases that are emotionally heavy,
where a singer is "leaning into" the text, or where the harmonic support is slow.

**Intra-phrase acceleration**: A phrase starts on time but accelerates through its duration,
finishing early. Usually indicates the singer's mental clock is faster than the tempo, or
that the phrase has been learned faster than the target tempo.

## Developing Rhythmic Precision

1. **Metronome work**: Practising difficult phrases against a click track forces confrontation
   with timing errors that a musical feel can paper over.

2. **Body pulse**: Tapping, clapping, or stepping the beat while singing engages the body's
   natural entrainment mechanisms and anchors timing physically.

3. **Phrase isolation**: Removing accompaniment context and singing the phrase alone, then
   returning to accompaniment, reveals whether timing errors are reactive (responding late
   to cues) or internal (inaccurate tempo sense).

4. **Recording comparison**: Singing into a DAW alongside the reference track makes timing
   errors immediately visible as waveform offset and audible as phase inconsistency.
