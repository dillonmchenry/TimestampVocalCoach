# Vibrato in Singing

Vibrato is a regular oscillation of pitch produced during sustained singing. In healthy vocal
technique it arises naturally from a free, well-supported tone and is not consciously produced.
Its perceptual qualities — rate, extent, and evenness — are closely tied to phonatory health
and technical development.

## Parameters of Healthy Vibrato

**Rate (Hz)**: The frequency of pitch oscillation. Research consistently places healthy vibrato
in the 4.5–7 Hz range, with the most aesthetically preferred range being 5–6.5 Hz
(Sundberg, 1994; Titze, 1994). Rates below 4 Hz produce a *wobble* quality; rates above 7 Hz
produce a *bleat* or *flutter* quality.

**Extent (cents peak-to-peak)**: The total pitch range of the oscillation. Typical healthy
extents range from 50–100 cents. Extents above 120 cents obscure the perceived pitch centre
and can sound out of control; extents below 25 cents produce a vibrato that listeners
struggle to perceive as such.

**Evenness**: Consistent rate and extent across notes and phrases is a marker of a
well-established technique. Random variation in rate or extent indicates that vibrato
is not yet automatic.

## Physiological Origins

Vibrato originates primarily from rhythmic subglottic pressure pulsing (approximately at
the vibrato rate) interacting with the mechanical response of the vocal folds. Sundberg
(1987) demonstrated that this pressure pulsing is a natural byproduct of supported phonation —
it arises when the laryngeal muscles are relaxed and the breath support is engaged.

Forced vibrato — where a singer consciously "wiggles" the jaw, tongue, or larynx — produces
irregular, visually obvious oscillation that listeners often find distracting. The characteristic
that separates natural from forced vibrato is its automaticity: a genuinely free vibrato
continues steadily without conscious management.

## Why Vibrato Disappears or Distorts

**Tight jaw or tongue root**: Tension in the structures immediately adjacent to the larynx
restricts the natural laryngeal movement associated with vibrato. The first intervention for
absent or uneven vibrato is typically jaw and tongue root release.

**Insufficient breath support**: Without adequate subglottic pressure, the pressure pulsing
mechanism cannot establish itself. Vibrato often returns when support increases.

**Over-adduction**: Pressing the vocal folds together too firmly dampers the natural oscillation.
Pharyngeal tension is a common cause.

**Anxiety or performance nerves**: Voluntary muscular activation in response to stress tends
to lock the larynx, suppressing vibrato. Vibrato that appears in practice but disappears on
stage almost always traces to this.

## Style-Specific Use

Vibrato is not universal across all styles. Classical singing uses it as the default phonatory
state for sustained notes. Contemporary pop, folk, and early music frequently use straight tone
(vibrato-free sustained tone) as the default, reserving vibrato for specific expressive moments.

When evaluating a singer's vibrato against a reference recording, the reference style context
is essential: vibrato absence in an early music piece is not a flaw, and wide vibrato in an
opera singer is within expected parameters.

## Vibrato and Intonation

Because vibrato oscillates around a pitch centre rather than sitting on it, there is always a
perceptual ambiguity: listeners perceive the average (centre) of the oscillation, not any
individual frame. This means a singer can be "in tune" by perception while individual pitch
frames show ±50 cents deviation from the target. Pitch accuracy metrics should account for
this by either averaging across the vibrato cycle or comparing to the vibrato-corrected centre.
